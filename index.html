0. How to read this plan

Phase 1 takes the truck from where it is today to a sorted, safe, weather-tight driver in roughly six months of weekend work: body off, frame blasted and coated, spring-under conversion at near-stock height on the 33s, front discs, all-new rear brakes, engine overhauled, 4-speed and Dana 20 resealed, new harness, both tanks, heater back in, floors repaired, everything under the body coated, Traveltop sealed. Exterior paint and the cabin are deliberately deferred.

Phase 2 is paint and interior, done body-on, whenever you decide the truck has earned it.

Conventions used throughout:

  • Steps are numbered because order matters. A step that says do not skip is load-bearing for a later step.
  • Torque values are for lubricated threads unless marked dry — that is how IH published them (IHPA forum, torque-specs thread). Where a figure could not be verified against the CTS-2302 service manual it is marked (verify in CTS-2302). Buy the manual first; it is line one of the parts list.
  • Callouts: GATE = a decision you make with data from the truck before spending money. VERIFY = something to confirm on the truck before ordering. SAFETY = a hazard that has actually hurt people doing this work.
  • Parts are listed at the start of the section that uses them. Each row gives phase, priority (H/M/L — key in §3), the item, a linked source (vendor · part number), and the price shown on the vendor page on 3 Sep 2026. "Verified" means the product page itself was fetched and matched; "unverified" means it came from a category page or search result — call before ordering those. Prices will drift; treat them as ±15%.

1.3 Verify before ordering — the five photographs

VERIFY — block pad: RESOLVED 4 Sep 2026

Stamped 4-196E 3722. Engine is the original 4-196. Carburetor: Michael recalls a Holley 1904 on the engine. That is consistent with a late-1966 196 — the 1904 was built in a big-bore (1-9/16") version for the 152 and 196 (scoutparts lists it for 1966–67 Scout 800s; the 1920 came later). A 1904 rebuild kit is therefore the right kit. Still read the list number on the airhorn so the kit's float/gaskets match, and confirm the throttle-body flange is the larger 196 pattern rather than a 152 carb on an adapter.

VERIFY — transmission identity. Three checks: (1) casting number on the case — 13-01 family = T-18, 13-09 = T-19, T18-1P/1301-174- sometimes appear on a T-98; (2) metal tag under one of the shifter-tower bolts, driver side; (3) definitive: jack one rear wheel, T-case in 2H, mark the driveshaft, count driveshaft turns per crank revolution in 1st — ~6.3 = wide-ratio T-18, ~6.4 = T-98, ~4.0 = T-18A/C or T-19A, ~5.1 = T-19. Also measure the input shaft: the 4-cyl bell takes a 15/16" 10-spline input; if yours is 1-1/8" or 1-1/4" someone fitted a V8-family box and the clutch kit changes. Photograph the transmission-to-T-case adapter plate and the bellhousing (IH 860014R1 is the hydraulic "pancake" 4-cyl bell).

VERIFY — axle ratios: RESOLVED 4 Sep 2026. Both axles stamped 4.27, confirmed by wheel-turn / driveshaft count. No regear; 33s give an effective ~3.68 final drive. Still to read: the transfer case serial tag (IHPA's Dana 20 kit splits at serial G435971 — the chassis serial suggests the early kit, but the tag on the case is what counts).

VERIFY — what the spring-over job left behind (not axle identity — that is settled). Four things decide the suspension order: (1) Resolved 4 Sep 2026: the original spring-under perches were removed — both axles need new weld-on perches; inspect the tube underside where they were cut off for gouges or grinder-thinned spots and locate the new perches clear of any damage. (2) Are the SOA perches welded to the tube or up against the cast center? That sets how they get cut off. (3) Shackle bushing sleeve width — the CPT kit is ordered as 1.875" (1961–67) or 2" (1968–71); a previous owner who changed hangers or shackles can have changed this, so measure the sleeve, don't assume. (4) Dana 27 tube OD with calipers at the perch location — no published figure exists, and the front U-bolts and perches are sized to it (the Dana 44 is 2.75"). Also look at the front driveshaft at the crossmember and the rear slip-yoke engagement.

ASSUMPTION — wheels (4 Sep 2026). The wheels are 15x8 (rim stamp 15X8 NT12 2 831; the stamp carries size only, not offset). Backspacing and center bore have not been measured; the plan proceeds on the assumption the existing wheels work. The assumption gets tested for free at §5.3 step 7 (caliper clearance) and §5.4 step 5 (fender clearance at full bump and lock) before anything is painted — if either fails, the fallback is a set of 15x8 steel wheels at 3.75" backspace, not trimming. Width isn't the deciding number — backspacing is, and generic 15x8 steelies run anywhere from 3.5" to 4.5". Wheel face down, straightedge across the back lip, tape to the hub mounting face. Target for 33x10.50 on a Dana 27: 3.75–4.0"; less rubs the fender lip at lock, more hits the tie rod and spring. Center bore must be ≥4.20" to clear the Warn hub body. Caliper clearance with the disc kit can only be checked by test-fitting (§5.3 step 7).

1.4 What the compression numbers say

Theoretical cranking pressure from the 196's 8.02:1 compression ratio is ~140 psi; IHPA's practical floor for a hot SV-family engine is 120 psi with no more than 10 psi spread. Your best wet reading (117) is under that floor and every dry reading is 55–65% of theoretical.

The wet gains are the diagnostic: #1 +18, #3 +16, #4 +22 — oil sealed those cylinders, so rings and bores are the leak path. #2 gained only 7 psi from the lowest base, so oil could not fix #2: that is a valve-seat or head problem, and on this engine family an IHPA moderator estimates more than half the heads he sees are cracked between a valve seat and the plug hole. #2 gets a Magnaflux and pressure test before any money goes into the head.

The cold 55–80% leakage figures overstate the truth — cold bores at minimum clearance with no oil film, hydraulic lifters that have bled down and may hold a valve a hair off its seat, and a very real chance the test was not at exact TDC-compression. Reasoned estimate for a warm test with all plugs out, throttle wide open, charged battery: ~105–115 psi on #1/#4, ~95–105 on #3, ~90–100 on #2, and warm leak-down in the 25–45% band on 1/3/4 with #2 still worst. That is still "overhaul likely" territory, not "tune-up" territory.

GATE 1 — engine scope

The warm test is worth doing only to confirm #2 as the head cylinder. The plan assumes a full overhaul (bore/hone to the next oversize, new pistons/rings, crank polish or grind, cam bearings, timing gears, valve job) because the wet gains say the bores are worn, not just the rings, and 0.020" cam-lobe wear is routine on these. The re-ring branch survives only if the machine shop measures bores within 0.005" taper/out-of-round and the crank within spec. Both paths are laid out side by side in §5.5.1 — read that before you spend anything on the engine, and decide with the block on the bench, not before.

5.1 Weeks 2–3: teardown to a bare frame

Order of removal (each step bags its own hardware, labeled):

  1. Interior. Subaru seats (unbolted — just lift). Shift boots and both shift levers (trans and twin-sticks) so the tub will clear later. Dash: unplug the loose harness at every point, photograph, and pull the whole aftermarket harness out — it is not being reused. Remove the instrument cluster you have already cleaned (store it wrapped). Pull the remaining tank: filler hose, vent, sender wire, fuel line, straps. Remove the tank compartment sheet metal only if rotted.
  2. Traveltop. Remove any fiberglass/insulation liner. Fold the visors down. Find the 5–7 bolts per side into the body flange (rear ones are the rustiest — penetrant for days, expect to cut one) and the 4 Phillips screws through the windshield header. Disconnect the dome/liftgate wiring at the frame connector (it pulls up through the fender). Three people: pop the front loose upward, block each side behind the doors with wood, lift and walk it to a pair of sawhorses padded with carpet. Store it upside down on the sawhorses, out of the sun. Count the bolts — it decides which Traveltop parts fit later.
  3. Doors. Off at the hinges (hinge screws are cheap; don't fight them). Store standing on their bottom edge on carpet.
  4. Glass. Windshield stays in the frame for now; it comes out in §6.9 when the cowl is being worked. Traveltop glass stays in the top until §8.3.
  5. Hood. Two people; mark the hinge positions with a scribe first.
  6. Engine and transmission as a unit (the pancake bell makes separating them in the truck miserable): drain oil, coolant, trans, and T-case. Radiator out (hoses, two bolts to the core support). Air cleaner, carb linkage, choke cable, fuel line, heater hoses (if any), exhaust at the manifold donut (bolts will snap — cut the pipe instead; the manifold bolt kit is on order), starter wiring, alternator, distributor wires, oil-pressure and temp senders, clutch slave (leave it on its hose, tie it up), speedometer cable at the T-case, both driveshafts (mark yoke orientation with paint before unbolting; bag the straps), rear trans mount, both engine mounts. Hoist with a load leveler; angle nose-up steeply to clear the T-case out under the firewall. Set the assembly on a pallet; separate T-case, then trans, then bell, then flywheel on the ground (bell bolts 105 ft-lb coming out — long breaker bar).
SAFETY — hoist

The engine-plus-T-18-plus-Dana 20 assembly is ~800 lb hanging well off-center. Use a leveler, chain to the head bolts through proper lifting eyes (not the manifold bolts), and never let anyone under it.

  1. Body off. Disconnect: steering column at the box coupler (the column stays with the tub), brake master cylinder from the firewall (leave it on the frame side if it's frame-mounted — on an 800 it's firewall-mounted, so unbolt the pedal pushrod and cap the lines), clutch master (same), parking brake cable at the lever, throttle linkage, wiring at the bulkhead (already gone), ground straps, heater/defroster ducts. Then the 9 body mount bolts: front center under the radiator core support, then four pairs down the tub. Bag each mount's shims and label them by position — they set the door gaps. Lift: bottle-jack front and rear of the tub separately, slide the 4×4s through crosswise under the rockers (never under the floor pans), lower the frame off the stands and roll it out, then lower the tub onto the sawhorses. Four people or two hoists; it is heavier than it looks.
  2. Strip the frame. Springs and shackles (photograph the SOA perches first — you may want the old front shackle geometry as a reference), both axles complete (roll them away on furniture dollies), steering box and drag link, brake lines and hoses (cut them; all new), pedal assembly, clutch/brake hydraulics, parking brake cross-shaft and cables, fuel lines, exhaust hangers, bumper stubs (cut off and grind flush the aftermarket brackets on the frame horns — the repro bumper uses the original bolt holes and stand-offs), the old body mount cups. What's left is a bare frame.
  3. Inspect the frame before it goes to the blaster: put a straightedge along each rail top; string diagonals from the front spring hanger to the opposite rear hanger — the two diagonals should match within 1/8". Look hard at the rear spring hangers, the front shackle hangers (they crack), the steering box mount, and the crossmember the front driveshaft passes. Poke every boxed section. Photograph the results.
GATE 2 — frame

If diagonals are out by more than ¼" or any hanger is cracked, that is a frame-shop job (straightening, hanger replacement) before blasting. Expect not to need it — you reported minimal rust — but check before you pay to blast.

5.5 Weeks 8–12: engine

5.5.1 The two engine paths

There are exactly two honest ways to do this engine, and the choice is not a matter of taste — it is decided by four measurements the machine shop takes with the block stripped on the bench. This section exists so you walk in already knowing what each answer costs you.

Before either path: settle the head. Your #2 cylinder gained only 7 psi wet from the lowest dry reading (§1.4), which is the signature of a valve-seat or head fault rather than rings — and cracks between a valve seat and the plug hole are common on this family. So #2’s head goes for Magnaflux and a pressure test first. Whatever it costs is common to both paths and must not be allowed to sway the block decision. Get that number, set it aside, then choose.

What the evidence currently says. The plan defaults to Path A, and it should: every dry reading is 55–65% of theoretical, the wet gains point at the bores, and 0.020" of cam-lobe wear is routine on these engines — which alone pulls a cam and cam bearings into the job. Path B is not a fallback for a tired engine; it is the right answer only for an engine whose bores turn out to be better than its compression test suggested. That happens, which is why you measure. It is simply not where the money would be.

When Path B genuinely wins. If the four measurements come back clean, this is a 4-cylinder in a truck that will see a few thousand miles a year, not a race engine. A re-ring on a straight bore is proper work, not a shortcut, and the $2,000 you keep buys the disc conversion and the harness outright. What to say at the machine shop

Hand them the block and ask for one thing before any work: "Measure and quote both ways. I need bore taper and out-of-round at top, middle and bottom of every cylinder; crank journal sizes and whether a polish will do; cam lobe lift against spec; and whether the deck is flat. Then quote me a bore-and-hone-to-.030 with the head done, and a hone-and-re-ring with the head done, as two separate numbers." Any shop that will not do that is the wrong shop. Do not order the overhaul kit until those numbers are in your hand — that is why the kit is tagged P1-gate.

Note the third option, so it is named and dismissed on purpose: leave the engine alone and drive it. With cold leak-down at 55–80% and every cylinder under the 120 psi practical floor, that means a truck that burns oil, fouls plugs and is down on power — while sitting on a fresh frame, new brakes and a new harness. It is your money, but it puts a worn engine in the middle of an otherwise sorted truck, and pulling it later means undoing §5.7.

Engine paint — the one piece of finish work that belongs in Phase 1

Everything cosmetic in this plan is deferred to Phase 2 on purpose. Engine paint is the exception, and not because it matters more: it is the only finish job whose cost multiplies if you wait. The engine is stripped on a stand in week 10, where painting it is three rattle cans and an afternoon. Deferring it means pulling the engine back out, which undoes §5.7. So it goes in now.

6.8 Electrical

GATE 6 — the electric fuel pump you already have

The truck currently runs an electric fuel pump, fitted by someone before you. Keeping it is the cheapest path — you own it — but it was installed to an unknown standard, and three things decide whether it stays. Check all three in week 1, while the engine is still in and running.

1. Pressure. Tee a gauge in at the carb and read it running. The Holley 1904 is a low-pressure carburettor: much above 6 psi and the float valve is pushed off its seat, which floods the carb, washes the bores and dumps fuel into the oil. An EFI-style pump fitted to a carburetted engine is a common previous-owner mistake. Over 6 psi means a regulator, or the pump goes.

2. A safety cut-off — this is the one that matters. Trace the pump's wiring. If it runs whenever the key is on, with nothing that stops it when the engine stops, fix that before the truck is driven again. A mechanical pump stops when the engine stops because the camshaft stops turning it; an electric pump does not know the difference between a stalled engine and a rolled one, and will happily empty a tank through a broken line onto a hot manifold. An oil-pressure safety switch or an inertia cut-off, with a start-bypass so it primes on cranking, is the standard answer and costs under $100.

3. Mounting and plumbing. These pumps push far better than they pull: it belongs low and near the tank, not on the firewall. Check for rubber fuel line that is not fuel-injection rated, unsupported runs, and hose clamps on a pressure line.

Then decide, before §5.5 assembly. Keeping it means the block's mechanical pump pad needs a block-off plate, and the SSS harness needs a fuel pump circuit — so this decision has to be made before the harness is ordered in week 2, alongside the wiper circuit from GATE 5. Going back to mechanical means the SP10172 pump and no wiring at all. If the pump passes all three checks, keep it: you own it, it primes the carb before cranking after a long sit, and it is not vulnerable to vapour lock the way a hot mechanical pump on this manifold can be.

GATE 5 — wipers: RESOLVED, electric conversion

Decision: electric wipers, mechanical fuel pump. You own a set of original vacuum motors, and keeping them was seriously considered — the write-up is kept below because it is reversible, and because the reasoning explains the fuel pump choice.

Why vacuum was on the table. Vacuum wipers earned their reputation on cars that fed them intake manifold vacuum, which collapses exactly when you open the throttle — wipers stop halfway up a hill in the rain. IH avoided that with a dual-action fuel pump (IHPA SL326086C93, $449.95 + $100 core) whose vacuum section keeps them moving under load. That pump is the entire reason the factory system worked.

Why electric won. Three things. The dual-action pump costs roughly $400 more than a plain mechanical one, so the vacuum path was never free. The Scout 800 has two motors (RH 306408C91, LH 306407C91) and a rebuild is $408.81 a side — two weak motors cost more than the whole $599.95 conversion, and you cannot know which until you test them. And an electric two-speed wiper simply works better: full speed at idle, unaffected by throttle, with a real switch instead of a vacuum valve.

The coupling worth understanding. An electric fuel pump does not rescue vacuum wipers — it makes fuel pressure, not vacuum, so the wipers would fall back to manifold vacuum and the bad behaviour returns. Vacuum wipers require the mechanical dual-action pump specifically. Since the wipers are now electric, the pump only has to pump fuel, and the cheapest, simplest option wins: the plain mechanical pump (SP10172). No wiring, no relay, no safety switch. An electric pump is listed as a gated option if you ever want one, but it buys nothing here and adds a fire-safety requirement.

Two things to do anyway. Tell SSS the harness needs a wiper circuit and switch feed — that is the opposite of what a vacuum truck needs, and the harness is ordered in week 2. And keep your vacuum motors: bag them labelled with the truck, since they are getting scarce and they make this decision reversible. If you ever want them, the parts are still tagged P1-gate in the tables — the dual-action pump, the rebuild service, and the hose.

7. Commissioning

  1. Fluids checklist: engine oil (15W-40 or a 10W-40 with zinc for cam break-in, plus a break-in additive), filter pre-filled; coolant; trans MT-1 80W-90; T-case same; front diff 85W-140; knuckles TK 777; rear diff GL-5 80W-90 + modifier; brake DOT 3; clutch DOT 3; steering box lube.
  2. Pre-start: plugs out, crank until oil pressure shows (or prime with a drill); fuel to the carb (rebuilt with your kit, float level per the manual); static timing; all grounds re-checked; fire extinguisher in reach.
  3. First start and cam break-in: start, immediately bring to 2,000–2,500 rpm and hold for 20 minutes varying ±300 rpm (never idle a new flat-tappet cam); watch oil pressure (50–60 cold, 10–20 hot idle minimum with SAE 30 is the factory floor), temperature, leaks. Shut down, re-torque the head after full cool-down if the gasket maker requires, re-check intake and exhaust bolts.
  4. Timing and carb: 4° BTDC at 500–575 rpm with vacuum disconnected, then check total advance ~34–36° at 3,000 rpm; idle mixture by vacuum gauge.
  5. Drivetrain: clutch free play, shift through all gears static, twin-sticks through 2H/4H/N/4L, hubs lock and unlock.
  6. Brakes: pedal height and free play; bed the pads (10 stops 30→5 mph, cool); then set the prop valve on a loose surface — fronts should lock a hair before the rears; re-bleed after the first 20 miles.
  7. Alignment: with the truck on its wheels and driven around the block to settle the springs: torque shackle and eye bolts at ride height; caster +3–4° (add/flip the CPT shims if not); toe-in 1/16–1/8" at tire centerline height; wheel centered with the box on its high spot. Re-torque U-bolts at 100 miles.
  8. Wheels: lug nuts 90–110 ft-lb, re-torque at 50 miles.
  9. Shakedown, 500 miles: 100-mile checks of every fluid level and leak; 300-mile oil and filter change (break-in oil out); drum re-adjust; parking brake re-set; kingpin preload re-check (new bearings seat); rear hub nut re-check (cotter pins out, confirm torque, new pins); driveshaft strap bolts; every ground.
